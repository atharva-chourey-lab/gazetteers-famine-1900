# Not rainfall: mean rainfall and population loss in the 1899–1900 famine — a famine-focused re-extraction of the Imperial Gazetteers for central India

Historical statistics · 2026

Author: Atharva Chourey
Affiliation: Atharva Chourey Lab (independent research lab)

*Manuscript — not yet peer-reviewed.*

## Abstract

Did tracts with kinder ordinary climates lose fewer people in the famine of 1899–1900? Across 34 units of central India — 19 Central Provinces districts, 4 Berar districts, and 11 Central India Agency princely states — the answer is no. Gazetteer-reported mean (long-run normal) rainfall is essentially uncorrelated with population change between the 1891 and 1901 censuses: *r* = −0.05 (*n* = 20). That null is this paper's headline result, and it is strictly a statement about *mean* rainfall — not about the 1899 rainfall deficit, which the prior literature does link to famine. We reach it through a famine-focused re-extraction of the Imperial Gazetteers: Roy (2014) first extracted a statistical panel from the 1908 Gazetteers; the lab re-extracts that source for one famine region, joining the census spine to the gazetteer-native famine series (relief, outturn, death-rates, prices, land out of cultivation). The panel also re-quantifies a contrast contemporaries already reported (Census of India 1901; Famine Commission 1901; Holderness's −14.5% for famine-affected native states): the 11 princely states lost an unweighted mean of −16.2%, against −7.5% for the 23 British districts; the four worst outcomes are all princely states (Bhopal −30.1%, Jaora −28.4%, Dewas −23.2%, Indore −22.7%). Mortality decouples from headcount wherever people could move (*r* = +0.46, *n* = 9, not significant); relief coverage tracks distress, not outcome (*r* = +0.30, *n* = 12, not significant). All results are descriptive: the decade sums two famines, and migration sits inside the outcome.

## Key results

- Headline null: gazetteer-reported mean (long-run normal) rainfall is essentially uncorrelated with population change between the 1891 and 1901 censuses — r = −0.05 (n = 20). The null is strictly about mean rainfall, not the 1899 deficit.
- Regime contrast, re-quantified: the 11 princely states lost an unweighted mean of −16.2% of population, against −7.5% for the 23 British districts — a contrast contemporaries already reported (Census of India 1901; Famine Commission 1901; Holderness's −14.5% for famine-affected native states).
- The four worst outcomes are all princely states: Bhopal −30.1%, Jaora −28.4%, Dewas −23.2%, Indore −22.7%.
- Mortality decouples from headcount wherever people could move (r = +0.46, n = 9, not significant); relief coverage tracks distress, not outcome (r = +0.30, n = 12, not significant).
- Roy (2014) first extracted a statistical panel from the 1908 Gazetteers; this paper is a famine-focused re-extraction for 34 units of central India.
