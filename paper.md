# Not rainfall: mean rainfall and population loss in the 1899–1900 famine — a famine-focused re-extraction of the Imperial Gazetteers for central India

Author: Atharva Chourey
Affiliation: Atharva Chourey Lab (independent research lab)

*Manuscript in preparation — not yet peer-reviewed.*

---

## Abstract

Did tracts with kinder ordinary climates lose fewer people in the famine of 1899–1900? Across 34 units of central India — 19 Central Provinces districts, 4 Berar districts, and 11 Central India Agency princely states — the answer is no. Gazetteer-reported mean (long-run normal) rainfall is essentially uncorrelated with population change between the 1891 and 1901 censuses: *r* = −0.05 (*n* = 20). That null is this paper's headline result, and it is strictly a statement about *mean* rainfall — not about the 1899 rainfall deficit, which the prior literature does link to famine. We reach it through a famine-focused re-extraction of the Imperial Gazetteers: Roy (2014) first extracted a statistical panel from the 1908 Gazetteers; the lab re-extracts that source for one famine region, joining the census spine to the gazetteer-native famine series (relief, outturn, death-rates, prices, land out of cultivation). The panel also re-quantifies a contrast contemporaries already reported (Census of India 1901; Famine Commission 1901; Holderness's −14.5% for famine-affected native states): the 11 princely states lost an unweighted mean of −16.2%, against −7.5% for the 23 British districts; the four worst outcomes are all princely states (Bhopal −30.1%, Jaora −28.4%, Dewas −23.2%, Indore −22.7%). Mortality decouples from headcount wherever people could move (*r* = +0.46, *n* = 9, not significant); relief coverage tracks distress, not outcome (*r* = +0.30, *n* = 12, not significant). All results are descriptive: the decade sums two famines, and migration sits inside the outcome.

**Keywords:** famine of 1899–1900; Central Provinces; Berar; Central India Agency; princely states; Imperial Gazetteer; Census of India; relief administration; historical demography

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 The question

Every famine poses two questions, and they are easily confused. The first is meteorological and agricultural: how badly did the rains fail, and how much of the harvest was lost? The second is demographic and political: given that failure, how many people did a place lose — and why did one place lose so many more than its neighbour? This paper is about the second question, asked of one region in one famine decade: the Central Provinces, Berar, and the princely states of the Central India Agency in the famine of 1899–1900, as recorded between the censuses of 1891 and 1901.

The distinction matters because the two questions can have different answers. A crop can fail almost completely and a population can nevertheless hold, if relief is organised early and at scale and people who flee can return or be replaced; conversely, a population can collapse where the harvest failure was no worse than its neighbour's. If outcomes tracked ordinary climate, the map of population loss would be a mean-rainfall map. The headline finding of this paper is that, in this region, it is not: mean rainfall and population loss are essentially uncorrelated. The map instead follows political boundaries — a pattern contemporaries already reported, which this paper re-quantifies on Gazetteer units rather than claims to discover (Section 1.3).

### 1.2 The famine in the literature, as the project records frame it

The underlying project records engage four named debates, and this paper inherits that framing rather than importing a new one.

First, the oldest organising contrast in famine studies: food availability decline against entitlement failure, associated in the records with Amartya Sen's entitlement approach. Where crop outturns survive in the sources consulted here, the availability failure in 1899–1900 was real and near-total (Section 3.5); there is no "famine amid plenty" in this region in these sources. But outcomes do not track outturn, and the records locate the entitlement margin in prices and in the composition of relief rather than in district headcounts. District aggregates, we stress throughout, cannot adjudicate that debate cleanly.

Second, the railways claim, associated in the records with McAlpin and with Burgess and Donaldson's district-level panel for British India (1875–1919; Burgess and Donaldson 2010), which codes annual rainfall, railroad penetration, and a 0–3 famine-intensity index from official reports and finds that railroads sharply reduced the mortality impact of rainfall shocks. Two precisions matter for everything that follows. That panel covers British districts only. And its rainfall variable is the annual rainfall *deficit or shock* — a different variable from the long-run *mean* rainfall examined in this paper; its strong deficit result neither contradicts nor is contradicted by our mean-rainfall null (Sections 3.2 and 3.6). Our sample — a single region, British districts and princely states together, with decadal net population change as the outcome — tests a different quantity, and Section 3.6 reports that the rail advantage does not replicate on this sample and outcome. The two results can both be true; they answer different questions.

Third, irrigation as insurance. The Famine Code's planning assumption, as recorded in the sources, priced relief works for 20% of a district's population as standard, but only 10% "in tracts adequately protected by irrigation" — the administration believed the insurance story. In this region in 1900 irrigation barely existed (Section 3.6), so the claim is untestable here, and that untestability is itself a finding.

Fourth, the measurement literature on famine intensity, in the spirit of Howe and Devereux's scales: a scale built from population change cannot then be used to "explain" population change. The dataset therefore scores each unit's distress independently of its demographic outcome (Section 2.4).

Running through all four debates is a fifth theme, which the 1901 Famine Commission itself stated in prose: migration distorts every district figure. Population change in a famine is a net figure — deaths, minus births, minus out-flight, plus in-flight — and in mobile tracts the migration terms can swamp the mortality term. Any reading of the 1891→1901 change as "mortality" is, for most units, simply wrong, and this paper does not make it.

### 1.3 What the Gazetteers offer — and what already exists

The sources are public-domain British-era publications, read on Internet Archive: the provincial *Imperial Gazetteer* volumes (Central Provinces, 1908; Central India, 1908; Berar, 1909), the Census of India volumes for 1901 and 1911, and the Famine Commission report of 1901. The Gazetteers' articles are where the famine lives in structured prose — relief peaks and their months, outturns, death-rates, prices, acres out of cultivation, crop-regime characterisations — while the censuses supply the population spine; neither source, alone, joins the two.

Three bodies of prior work bound what can be claimed here, and the lab states the bounds plainly, following a precedent sweep of this paper's framings (adopted in full; see the accompanying sources map). **First, the Gazetteers have been extracted before.** Roy (2014) processed a dataset from the 25-volume *Imperial Gazetteer of India* (1908) — area, population, revenue, rainfall, cultivated and irrigated areas, roads, railway network, literacy, and largest town, for 430 districts of British India and 130 princely states — a decade before this project. Roy's extraction is a c.1908 cross-section built to study regional inequality, not a famine panel; this paper is a **famine-focused re-extraction and re-use** of the Gazetteer data for one region and one famine decade, and claims no priority of extraction. **Second, the princely–British contrast was published contemporaneously.** The Census of India 1901 report (Risley and Gait 1903) and the Famine Commission (1901) documented heavier losses and inadequate or delayed relief in the Native States, mass immigration from the states into British districts, and a fall of −4.92% in affected British districts; Holderness's census-based calculation put famine-affected native states at −14.5% (from 42 million in 1891 to 36 million in 1901), while unaffected states rose over 12%; contemporary estimates put deaths at about 3 million in the native states against about 1 million in British territory (as reported in *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, 1911, "Famine"). Section 3.3 therefore presents our −16.2% against −7.5% strictly as a **re-quantification** of that contemporaneously reported contrast on Gazetteer units for this region — not as a discovery. **Third, two structured resources already exist.** The census population spine exists as data on modern district footprints in the `censusindia` resource (Saket Lab; Jolad and Singh 2026, Harvard Dataverse, doi:10.7910/DVN/ON8CP8), used here as a cross-check (Section 2.5); it does not contain 1891 values, and the princely states as units do not exist in it — its "Indore" is the modern Indore district (population 302,057 in 1901), not Indore State (850,690). Burgess and Donaldson's panel likewise covers British districts by construction, rainfall records being unavailable for the Native States. What survives the sweep as this paper's headline is a single, narrow result: across a Gazetteer panel that includes the princely states, 1891–1901 population change is essentially uncorrelated with Gazetteer-reported **mean** rainfall (*r* = −0.05). No prior work located by the sweep reports that correlation, for that variable, outcome, and sample.

### 1.4 Roadmap and headline results

Section 2 describes the extraction, unit construction, variables, and comparison design. Section 3 presents the results in headline order: the panel (3.1); the mean-rainfall null (3.2); the regime contrast, re-quantified (3.3); mortality decoupling (3.4); relief, outturn, and prices (3.5); railways and irrigation (3.6); and a robustness check from the companion 1872–1941 panel (3.7). Section 4 discusses interpretation and states the design's limits explicitly, including what it cannot show. Three results preview the argument:

1. **The headline: mean rainfall.** Gazetteer-reported mean (normal) rainfall against population change: *r* = −0.05 (*n* = 20) — no relationship in either direction worth the name. This is a statement about mean rainfall only, not about the 1899 deficit.
2. **Re-quantified background: regime.** Princely states: −16.2% mean population change, 1891→1901 (*n* = 11). British districts: −7.5% (*n* = 23). The 8.7-point gap re-quantifies, on Gazetteer units, a contrast reported contemporaneously (Section 1.3); it is not presented as a new finding.
3. **Decoupling.** The two highest recorded district death-rates belong to districts whose populations *grew*. Severity, mortality, and headcount loss are three different things in this famine, and the paper keeps them separate.

## 2. Data and methods

### 2.1 Sources and extraction

All values were read by hand from digitised public-domain publications held on Internet Archive, curated into a per-unit dataset (`districts.csv`) in which every value carries a named source and, where relevant, a flag. Nothing was interpolated. The primary sources, as named in the project records, are:

- **Population, Central Provinces and Berar districts:** Census of India 1911, Vol. X, Table II, which back-computes 1891 (and 1881) populations on 1911 boundaries, cross-checked against Census of India 1901, Central Provinces Report, Statement I.
- **Population, Central India states:** Census of India 1901, Vol. XIX and Vol. XIX-B (Central India; Superintendent C. S. Luard, cited in the records as Luard) and the *Imperial Gazetteer of India*, Provincial Series: Central India (1908), state articles.
- **Famine series:** the district and state articles of the *Imperial Gazetteer of India*, Provincial Series — Central Provinces (1908), Central India (1908), and Berar (1909) — supplemented by the Report of the Famine Commission (1901), cited by paragraph in the records (notably paras 15, 16, 183–186).
- **Natural divisions:** Russell's divisions in the Census of India 1901, Central Provinces Report, Chapter II, used for the basin/division coding in the dataset.

A map-ready extract (`atlas_layer.csv`) and seven figures accompany the dataset; every figure's numbers are in `districts.csv`. Computations are in `analysis.py`; the curated data construction is in `build_data.py`. Re-running `analysis.py` regenerates both CSVs, all figures, and the statistics quoted in this paper.

### 2.2 Unit construction

The panel has **34 units**: 19 Central Provinces districts, 4 Berar districts, and 11 Central India Agency princely states (Indore, Bhopal, Rewa, Datia, Dhar, Dewas Senior and Junior combined as a single row, Jaora, Ratlam, Chhatarpur, Charkhari, Barwani). Regime coding is binary and constant across the decade: British-administered district (*n* = 23) versus princely state (*n* = 11). Two coding notes from the records apply. Berar was Hyderabad territory leased in perpetuity and British-administered throughout (assigned to the Central Provinces in 1903); it is coded British. Sambalpur was a Central Provinces district until its transfer to Bengal in 1905; it is coded British. No unit changed regime in the decade.

Boundaries are the single most consequential construction choice, and the famine dataset and the companion long panel make different ones, deliberately. The famine dataset uses **1911 boundaries** for Central Provinces and Berar districts (the 1911 census back-computation) and **1901 boundaries** for the states. Berar rows use the post-1905 map (Ellichpur merged into Amraoti; Basim into Akola; Wun renamed Yeotmal). Two rows split a single 1901 district: Raipur excluding Drug, and Drug (a district only from 1906) on its 1911-boundary back-series; relief for both was reported under undivided Raipur, so the printed relief percentage is retained rather than recomputed across mismatched bases. The companion panel (Section 3.7) instead uses the latest-adjusted figure for each census year (1931-volume adjustments for 1881–1931), so the same district-year can differ slightly between the two projects; the differences are quantified in the panel's report (see Section 3.7).

Six state percentage changes exist in the sources only as density-derived figures, with no 1891 total extracted: Rewa, Dewas (combined), Ratlam, Chhatarpur, Charkhari, and Barwani. They are flagged in the dataset and in Table 1 below, and the analysis code carries them as explicit overrides rather than computed values. One further derived value is flagged: Sambalpur's 1891 population is back-computed from the Famine Commission's stated +4.19% change (para 184), not read from a census table. Derived cells are never mixed silently with enumerated ones.

### 2.3 Variables

**Outcome.** Percentage population change, 1891→1901, per unit: 100 × (pop1901 − pop1891) / pop1891, computed from the totals above except for the six density-derived state values. The census years are 1891 and 1901 throughout; the famine of 1899–1900 falls at the end of that decade, which also contains the famine of 1896–97 — a confound Section 4 confronts directly.

**Trend-adjusted deficit.** For the 25 units with an 1881 count, the dataset also computes an excess-deficit ("missing people") measure: each unit's 1901 population is projected from its own 1881→1891 growth rate, and the shortfall of the actual count against that projection is expressed as a percentage of the projection. This measures the whole decade against trend, not the single famine (Section 3.1).

**Normal rainfall.** Long-run normal rainfall in inches per year, as printed in the Gazetteer articles and census retrospect, available for 20 of 34 units. It is a *normal* — ordinary climate — not a measure of the 1899 shock. Its definitions are heterogeneous across units, as the dataset flags record: station averages (for example Amraoti 33.96 in and Akola 33.69 in, 25-year averages ending 1901, from the Berar station tables), the rice-belt mean of 55 in applied to Bhandara, Raipur, and Drug, decadal averages for the states (Bhopal ~45 in; Barwani 20.9 in; Indore ~26.5 in, the mean of the state-note station series 1891–98), and single-station prose figures (Mandla 59 in). Rainfall *in 1899* survives in the sources consulted for only four units — Indore (7.58 in), Bhopal (33 in), Ratlam (15 in), and Barwani (7.26 in) — so no cross-unit correlation with the 1899 shock itself can be computed from these records, and none is reported. District-level 1899 rainfall by unit is **not reported in the underlying project records** beyond those four cases.

**Mortality.** District death-rates for 1900 (per mille), from the Famine Commission (paras 184 and 186), available for 9 units, plus province-level famine-period rates (Section 3.4). British India registered deaths; the princely states, except Ratlam and Barwani, did not — so unit-level mortality exists for British districts only, which is itself a finding about what the archive can see. Ratlam's famine-year rate (56 per mille, against 20 in ordinary years) is reported by Luard from state registration; Barwani, which kept registration from 1897, registered 6,900 deaths "due to famine sickness", about 9% of its enumerated population. No death-rate exists for the other nine states, and none is estimated.

**Relief coverage.** Peak share of a unit's population on relief works or gratuitous relief, with the peak month, extracted for 12 units — all British districts — from the Gazetteers' district articles. Percentages follow the Gazetteers' convention (on 1891 population) where flagged. No relief series for any princely state is reported in the underlying project records; the Famine Commission was expressly forbidden to enquire into relief administration in Native States.

**Crop outturn.** Harvest outturn in 1899–1900 as a percentage of normal (the administration estimated each harvest in annas of sixteen). A provincial figure survives for the Central Provinces (25% of normal) and district values for four units (Section 3.5). **Prices.** A single province-level series survives — Berar jowar in seers per rupee, from the Berar Gazetteer's Prices section. District-level price multiples **do not survive in the sources consulted** and are not reported. **Land out of cultivation.** Shares of cultivated area thrown out of cultivation in the famine year are carried in the dataset for two states — Indore 25.7% and Ratlam 37.5%. The project report additionally records Barwani at one-third (with Ali Rajpur, not a table row, likewise one-third), and a decade change in cultivated area for Bhopal (a shrinkage of 50–60% across the decade) that is deliberately kept out of the famine-year column because it is not a famine-year figure. **Rail access.** A binary coding of whether a railway served the unit by 1900, from the Gazetteer articles and railway history as recorded per row (Damoh coded "marginal": the Bina–Katni line reached it only in 1899, during the famine itself; the analysis treats values beginning "Yes" as rail-served). **Crop regime.** A qualitative characterisation per unit from the Gazetteer articles (wheat, rice, cotton and juar, hill millets, and so on). A numeric cotton share survives for only one unit (Wardha, 36% of its four listed principal crops), so crop-regime comparisons are presented as group means, not as a share regression.

### 2.4 The intensity classification

Each unit is classed 1–5 on **distress evidence** — crop failure, relief coverage, and mortality — *excluding* population change, the outcome to be explained. Class 5 (extreme): Bhopal, Jaora, Dewas, Indore (4 units). Class 4 (severe): 16 units. Class 3 (moderate-severe): 9 units. Class 2 (mild): 5 units, including Chhatarpur and Charkhari scored for 1899–1900 specifically (their decade declines belong mainly to 1896–97; see Section 3.1). Class 1 is unused. Per-unit rationale: the dataset's `intensity_basis` column. The classification is the lab's adaptation, not a contemporary official scale.

### 2.5 Verification

Three checks are recorded. First, **anchors**: population-weighting the Central Provinces British districts reproduces the Gazetteers project's verified anchor (−9.2% computed here on 1911 boundaries against −9.3% on 1901 boundaries), and the 11 states' unweighted mean (−16.2%) sits against Luard's all-Central-India density fall of −16.2% and the −16.4% anchor recorded in the project files. The Indore anchors check out in the primary source exactly: 1899 rainfall at Indore 7.58 inches, and 386,764 of 1,504,636 cultivated acres (25.7%) out of cultivation in the famine year. Second, **cross-check against `censusindia`** (doi:10.7910/DVN/ON8CP8): where a modern district footprint nearly coincides with the 1901/1911 unit, the two sources agree closely — Narsinghpur 315,518 and Betul 287,807 identical, the other eight compared units within ±0.6% — attributable to boundary back-projection choices, not transcription error (full list in the sources map). Third, the companion panel's transcription was verified against the census tables' own printed division and province totals; its checksums caught and fixed four values, two of which also correct the famine dataset (notably Narsinghpur 1891: 368,932 on the panel basis against 363,932 in the famine extraction; its famine-decade change, −13.3%, is reported on the famine basis, with the discrepancy flagged in the sources map).

### 2.6 Comparison design

The design is deliberately modest: descriptive comparison of unit-level aggregates. The headline quantity is the correlation between mean rainfall and population change; the unweighted mean population change by regime is reported as a re-quantification of a contemporaneously published contrast (Sections 1.3 and 3.3). Supporting quantities are Pearson correlations, crop-regime group means, a Welch *t*-test and within-group rainfall slopes for the rail comparison, and medians with interquartile ranges from the companion long panel. No multivariate regression is estimated: with *n* = 34 (*n* = 9–20 for the famine series), heterogeneous definitions, and migration inside the outcome, the records support descriptive geometry, not inference. Consistent with the project report, **none of the correlations reported is statistically significant at conventional levels**. Ecological-inference caveats are treated in Section 4.

Two features of the ranked table discipline everything that follows. First, the top of the table — the catastrophic tail — is exclusively princely: the four class-5 units are the four worst outcomes, and they are all Central India states. The worst British district, Saugor (−20.4%), sits fifth. Second, the bottom of the table refutes any simple severity reading: three of the four growing units (Akola, Sambalpur, Nimar) carry class 4, "severe", on distress evidence. Distress and demographic outcome are different things, and the table's bottom is where that difference is largest.

The trend-adjusted deficit deepens almost every loss, because the 1880s were a growth decade. Computable for 25 of 34 units, the worst deficits are Jaora −33.8% (raw change −28.4%), Bhopal −29.9%, Dhar −26.2% (raw −16.1%; Dhar had grown 13.6% in the 1880s), Bilaspur −26.1%, Indore −25.9%, Drug −24.6%, Balaghat −24.3%, and Saugor −24.1%. Warning case: Mandla's deficit is −17.2% although the Gazetteer says it was "not severely affected" in 1899–1900 — most of its shortfall is 1896–97 and trend arithmetic. The deficit measures the decade against trend, not the single famine.

### 3.2 The rainfall null: the headline result

Mean rainfall explains essentially nothing — and the scope of that sentence is the result. The variable is the Gazetteers' long-run *normal* (mean) rainfall: ordinary climate, not how far 1899 fell short in that unit. Nothing in this section speaks to the 1899 deficit, which the prior literature — using annual deficits and shocks, a different variable — does link to famine (Burgess and Donaldson 2010; Mishra et al. 2019, as located by the precedent sweep). Across the 20 units with a normal-rainfall value, the correlation between normal rainfall and population change is ***r* = −0.05** — indistinguishable from zero, and not statistically significant. The endpoints make the point without statistics. Balaghat, the province's wettest district at 63 inches, lost −14.9%. Dry Barwani, at 20.9 inches, lost only −4.5% enumerated (density-derived). Within the rail comparison (Section 3.6), the loss-versus-rainfall slope is approximately zero in both groups (rail +0.05, no-rail −0.09 percentage points per inch).

The four units with a surviving 1899 rainfall figure show that the shock itself was severe where it can be seen — Indore received 7.58 inches against a ~26.5-inch norm (28.6% of normal), Barwani 7.26 inches (34.7% of normal), Ratlam 15 inches against 34 (44.1%), Bhopal 33 inches against a ~45-inch decadal average (73.3%) — but four points cannot carry a cross-unit test, and the lab does not pretend otherwise. The null reported here is therefore precise in scope: *ordinary climate*, as the Gazetteers state it, does not sort the losses. A test using district-level 1899 rainfall deviations for all units is **not possible from the underlying project records**, because those deviations are not reported there beyond the four cases above (see also Section 4.2).

### 3.3 The regime contrast, re-quantified

This contrast is **not a new finding**, and the paper does not present it as one. Its direction and rough magnitude were published contemporaneously: the Census of India 1901 report (Risley and Gait 1903) and the Famine Commission (1901) documented the heavier losses in the Native States, and Holderness's census-based calculation put famine-affected native states at −14.5% (42 million in 1891 to 36 million in 1901), against −4.92% in affected British districts in the Commission's accounting. What this section adds is only the exact arithmetic on this paper's Gazetteer units — a re-quantification, stated so the headline null of Section 3.2 can be read against the region's best-known pattern.

On those units, the eleven princely states lost an unweighted mean of **−16.2%**; the twenty-three British districts lost **−7.5%**. Every one of the 11 states fell; the British districts ranged from Saugor's −20.4% to Nimar's +14.3%. The contrast survives the obvious disaggregations in the records. By crop-regime group, the six Malwa plateau states (Indore, Bhopal, Jaora, Dhar, Dewas, Ratlam) averaged **−21.1%** — the worst group in the region — against −9.7% for the rice tract (7 units), −9.7% for the Central Provinces wheat-and-mixed group (10 units), and −1.4% for the cotton tract (Berar's four districts plus Wardha and Nimar, 6 units). The Malwa figure is not one outlier carrying a mean: its members are −30.1%, −28.4%, −23.2%, −22.7%, −16.1%, and −6.1% (Ratlam, whose exceptional mortality-without-headcount-loss pattern is dissected in Section 3.4).

What differed across the boundary, on the records, was administration. British districts had a Famine Code, a relief programme costed in advance (works for 20% of the population as standard), and a census-and-registration apparatus. The states had durbar relief of unknown scale — unknown because the Famine Commission was expressly forbidden to enquire into relief administration in Native States, and no relief-coverage figure for any state is reported in the underlying project records. Two caveats, both from the records, qualify the gap's size but not, on these numbers, its existence. Luard warned that the 1891 Central India returns were probably too high, so state declines are somewhat overstated by an unknown amount. And the states were also, on average, the drier Malwa-plateau economies (Section 3.2 and Pattern 5 of the project report). The gap — roughly −16% against −7.5% — is too large to be only an enumeration artefact, but its exact size cannot be certified. Section 3.7 shows the same ordering on independently based medians over a seventy-year panel.

### 3.4 Mortality and population change decouple wherever people could move

At province level, the Famine Commission's rates establish that mortality was severe in both British provinces. The Central Provinces death-rate for the famine period November 1899–October 1900 was **54.37 per mille** against a decennial average of 34.50 (calendar 1900: 56.68 per mille). Berar's famine-period rate was **82.1 per mille** against 38.8 — more than double its average, and far above the Central Provinces rate, although Berar's population fell much less.

At district level the decoupling is systematic. The two highest district death-rates recorded for 1900 — Nimar, 105.0 per mille, and Sambalpur, 103.9 per mille — belong to districts whose populations *grew* (+14.3% and +4.2%). Basim recorded 110.8 per mille (the region's highest district rate, for the pre-1905 Basim district carried inside the combined Akola row, whose population was flat at +0.8%); Buldana 95.4 per mille (population −9.5%); Chanda 91.9 (−14.6%); Wardha 85.1 (−3.9%); Chhindwara 83.6 (+0.1%); Betul 81.4 (−11.9%). Across the nine units with death-rates, the correlation between mortality and population change is *positive* (*r* = +0.46, *n* = 9, not significant). The sign is the finding: higher recorded mortality does not predict larger headcount loss in this cross-section at all.

The Famine Commission saw the mechanism and said so. Nimar's rates are, in its treatment, misleading, because immigrants swelled the true population far above the 1891 base on which the rates were calculated: deaths were registered on residents *and* refugees, while the census counted whoever was present in March 1901, after colonisation of the Charwa tract and in-migration had refilled the district. The Commission's own figure for Nimar's increase, +14.37% since 1891 (para 184), is reproduced independently by our table at +14.3%. Ratlam shows the same mechanics inside a princely state: its death-rate rose from 20 per mille in ordinary years to 56 in the famine year, 37.5% of its cultivated land went out of cultivation, yet its enumerated population fell only −6.1% (density-derived) and Ratlam town *grew* from 29,822 to 36,321 — flight to the town and the railway, counted in both registers. Barwani registered 6,900 famine-sickness deaths, about 9% of its enumerated population, against an enumerated change of only −4.5%.

### 3.5 Relief, outturn, and prices

**Relief coverage measured distress, not demographic failure.** Peak relief coverage, extracted for 12 British-district units, runs from 3.6% (Wun, June 1900) to 45% (Betul, August 1900). The full extracted set is: Betul 45%, undivided Raipur 44% (printed figure, August 1900), Akola-proper ~39%, Nimar 31% (July 1900), Bilaspur ~29%, Hoshangabad 24%, Amraoti with Ellichpur ~23%, Buldana ~23%, Chanda ~22%, Saugor ~15%, Jubbulpore ~9%, Wun 3.6%. For scale, the Central Provinces Chief Commissioner put the province's peak at ~20% of population relieved. The correlation between relief coverage and population outcome is, if anything, positive (*r* = +0.30, *n* = 12, not significant) — the wrong sign for any naive reading in which more relief marks a worse famine outcome. The clearest case is Chhattisgarh: undivided Raipur put a printed 44% of its people on relief with an outturn of one-sixth of normal, and Bilaspur ~29%, yet Raipur-proper lost only −2.5%, and the Gazetteer says of Bilaspur, in terms, that "owing to the complete and timely organization of relief measures the mortality was not severe". Conversely Saugor, with ~15% coverage, lost −20.4%. The Commission had already warned (para 16) that relief numbers are not "the only or proper measure of the pressure of a famine"; our table adds the complement — nor are they a measure of its demographic outcome. No corresponding statement can be made for the states: their relief coverage is **not reported in the underlying project records** (Section 2.3).

**Outturn: the availability failure was real.** The Central Provinces' provincial outturn for 1899–1900 was **25% of normal** — in the Gazetteer's words, "the most complete failure of the rainfall ever known; only five districts received more than half their average rainfall, and five received only a third". District values extracted are undivided Raipur one-sixth (~17%), Wardha 25%, Betul 30%, and Narsinghpur 40%; qualitatively, both harvests were destroyed outright in Hoshangabad, Chanda, Bilaspur (rice), Nimar, Ratlam, and Barwani ("no crop of any kind"). But outcomes do not track outturn: Narsinghpur harvested two-fifths of normal and was, in the Gazetteer's phrase, "not severely distressed", yet lost −13.3%; Raipur harvested one-sixth and lost −2.5% (Raipur-proper). Availability failure was, on these records, necessary context — and nowhere near sufficient to predict the loss.

**Prices: the entitlement margin, visible at province level only.** Berar jowar, in seers per rupee (Imperial Gazetteer, Berar, Prices section): 23 in 1895–6 (normal), 11⅝ in 1896–7, 27.5 in 1898–9, ~15 on average in 1899–1900, 11.1 in 1900–1, and 5–7 in the worst months of early 1900. Inverted, jowar cost **3.3 to 4.6 times** its normal price at the peak, and roughly twice normal across the famine year — a collapse of the wage-goods exchange entitlement for anyone who buys food. District-level price series **do not survive in the sources consulted**; this is the thinnest of the famine measures, and the paper builds nothing further on it.

**Land out of cultivation.** Indore threw 386,764 of 1,504,636 cultivated acres — **25.7%** — out of cultivation in the famine year, rising to 68.9% in Rampura zila (193,449 of 280,893 acres). Ratlam lost **37.5%** of its cultivated area. These state figures have no British-district counterpart series in the records, and are reported as state-level corroboration of distress intensity, not as a cross-regime comparison.

**The cotton tract's famine hides in other registers.** The cotton group averaged only −1.4% population change, the mildest of any crop-regime group — yet Berar's famine-period death-rate (82.1 per mille) was half again the Central Provinces' (54.4 per mille), Basim's 110.8 per mille was the region's highest district rate, Amraoti's gratuitous relief (86,737 persons) exceeded its works relief (52,644) — the signature of a destitute relief load that could not labour — and estimated cattle mortality ran from 28% (Buldana) to 60% (Ellichpur's Melghat). A commercialised cotton economy could hold and re-attract people through the census while the famine killed residents, livestock, and savings. Judging Berar by population change alone would call its famine mild. It was not.

### 3.6 Railways and irrigation

**Railways: not replicated on this sample and outcome.** Rail-served units averaged **−10.5%** (*n* = 22); non-rail units averaged **−10.1%** (*n* = 12); the difference is null (Welch *t*-test, *p* = 0.89). The loss-versus-rainfall slope is approximately zero in both groups (rail +0.05, no-rail −0.09 points per inch), so there is no gradient here for rail to flatten. The princely states supply the decisive counterexamples to any naive version of the railways claim: Jaora, Bhopal, Indore, and Dewas were all rail-served and all catastrophic, while no-rail Dhar (−16.1%) did *better* than rail-served Jaora (−28.4%). Whatever railways did to grain-price volatility (Burgess and Donaldson's mechanism), on this evidence they did not protect populations where relief administration was a princely state's. The original result is untouched: different outcome (mortality/intensity against net headcount), different sample, different horizon (annual against decadal).

**Irrigation: untestable, and that is the finding.** Irrigation has no variance to explain anything with. Saugor irrigated ~3,000–5,000 acres in total, Hoshangabad ~4,000 acres of spring crops, Wun under 20 square miles, Amraoti under 5 square miles. The Gazetteer says flatly that irrigation was "as yet in its infancy in the Province". The Famine Code's planning discount for irrigated tracts (Section 1.2) therefore had almost no irrigated tract to apply to here. No irrigation effect is estimated.

### 3.7 Robustness: the famine decade in the seventy-year panel

The companion project follows the same 34 units across every all-India census the sources honestly support (1872 in part, then 1881–1941), on a different boundary basis (latest-adjusted figures; Section 2.2), and codes regime identically. Its famine-decade results, computed by its analysis script, corroborate the re-quantified regime contrast without depending on the famine dataset's boundary choices: in 1891–1901 the **median** British district changed by **−9.5%** (interquartile range −12.3% to −2.9%) against a **median** princely state change of **−14.0%** (IQR −22.8% to −8.6%); regime means on that basis are British −7.5% and states −15.9%, the small difference from −16.2% being the boundary basis plus one correction, as the panel report documents. Every state fell in the famine decade on that basis too (best: Barwani, −5.1% on panel absolutes against −4.5% density-derived in the famine dataset — a flagged basis difference).

The longer panel adds one interpretive fact the famine dataset alone cannot supply: **the princely penalty is shock-linked, not a constant drag.** In the ordinary decade before the famine (1881–91) there was no penalty — medians +8.8% (British) against +6.5% (princely), with overlapping spreads. The states then recovered more slowly after the famine (1901–11 medians +14.1% against +17.7%) and lagged again in the ordinary 1920s (1921–31: +6.8% against +11.5%). In levels, indexed aggregates (balanced subsets, 1881 = 100) reach 140.8 (British) against 124.9 (states) by 1941; the princely aggregate fell below its own 1881 level in 1901 (87.2) while the British aggregate held at 100.3, and the states' aggregate did not pass its 1891 level until 1931, the districts by 1911. In 1921, 8 of 11 states were still below their 1891 population, against 8 of 22 districts. The famine decade is where the two regimes' trajectories separate; the separation then persists (panel description, not an additional causal claim).

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Interpretation

The headline result is a null, and it should be read narrowly: a tract's *ordinary* climate, as the Gazetteers report it, carried no information about how much population it would lose in the famine decade. Ordinary climate is not destiny — which is precisely why the deficit literature and this paper do not conflict: Burgess and Donaldson (2010) show annual rainfall *shortages* raised famine intensity; we show long-run *means* do not sort decadal headcount loss across British districts and princely states together. Against that null, the re-quantified regime contrast (−16.2% against −7.5%, Section 3.3) and the relief pattern (Section 3.5) are consistent with the contemporaneous administrative reading — codified relief, costed in advance, blunted demographic loss where it operated — but consistency is not identification, and the lab claims no more. The decoupling results (Section 3.4) carry the paper's methodological moral: in this famine, distress, mortality, and headcount loss are three variables, not one, and any single-scale account will misdescribe at least one of Berar (a mortality event), Malwa (a demographic-collapse event), and Chhattisgarh (a relief-administration success) at a time.

### 4.2 Limitations — what this design cannot show

1. **Ecological inference.** Every number is a district aggregate over very unequal tracts. Correlations at *n* = 9–20 describe the map, not mechanisms, and none is statistically significant. Nothing here licenses household-level claims.
2. **Two famines, one decade.** The 1891→1901 change sums the famines of 1896–97 and 1899–1900; Bundelkhand was, in Luard's words, "not touched by the famine of 1899–00", yet Chhatarpur fell −15.0% and Charkhari −13.4%. The Famine Commission itself held that a single year's effect could not be estimated from the 1891–1901 change. This design cannot separate the two famines, and does not.
3. **Migration is inside the outcome.** Population change is natural change plus net migration. For most Central India states there is no vital registration, so deaths and net out-flight **cannot be separated**: a state's −25% is the two in unknown proportions, and calling it "mortality" would be wrong.
4. **Census quality.** Luard warned the 1891 Central India returns were probably too high, overstating state declines by an unknown amount; six state changes are density-derived, not enumerated (Section 2.2).
5. **Unit heterogeneity and boundaries.** District and state rows rest on different boundary bases (1911 against 1901), several districts changed territory materially (Chanda, Raipur/Drug), and rainfall "normals" mix station averages, belt means, and decadal averages (Section 2.3).
6. **Missing series.** District-level 1899 rainfall deviations, district price series, princely-state relief coverage, and princely-state mortality (beyond Ratlam and Barwani) are **not reported in the underlying project records**. The deficit test, the price test, and any relief-based explanation of the states' losses are therefore beyond this dataset — stated explicitly, not papered over.
7. **No causal claim.** Regime is not randomly assigned; the states were also the drier Malwa economies on average. The design shows *that* outcomes sort by boundary and not by mean rainfall. It cannot show that administration *caused* the difference, and the paper does not claim that it does.

### 4.3 What would strengthen this

The project records name the agenda: the Famine Commission's provincial volumes and the Central Provinces and Berar famine reports proper (relief series for the 22 units lacking them; district price series); district Gazetteers for 1899 rainfall by district and tahsil-level outturns; annual vital-registration abstracts; Burgess and Donaldson's panel joined at district level, so the deficit test can be run properly — annual shocks, not decadal normals — on a sample including the states; and the 1901 census birth-place tables, to decompose population change into natural change and net migration.
